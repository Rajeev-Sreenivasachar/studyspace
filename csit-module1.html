Introduction to Personal Computers

People prepare for IT careers through certifications, formal education, internships, and work experience. This module introduces computer cases, motherboards, power supplies, CPUs, RAM, expansion cards, storage drives, connectors, ports, and cables.

Why safety comes first

Computer equipment can contain hazards that cause electrical fires, injuries, or fatalities. ESD—Electrostatic Discharge—can damage equipment even when you cannot feel it.

The module finishes with hands-on disassembly so students can identify internal components and understand how they connect.

1.1

Personal Computer Safety

Electrical safety

  • Follow electrical guidelines to prevent fires, injuries, and fatalities.
  • Printer power supplies and other components can contain or retain high voltage after shutdown.
  • AC adapters are designed for specific devices; mismatches can damage both.
  • Grounding provides fault current a low-resistance path away from equipment. Server racks and large equipment also require grounding.

ESD

  • ESD happens when built-up static contacts a differently charged surface.
  • A person feels ESD at about 3,000 V; a painful/noisy shock may exceed 10,000 V.
  • Less than 30 V can damage a component.
  • Self-ground by touching a grounded object first.
  • Use antistatic bags, grounded workbench/floor mats, and wrist straps.
1.2

PC Components

The motherboard connects the system; the power supply delivers usable DC power; the CPU executes instructions; memory holds active data; storage preserves files; adapter cards add capabilities; ports connect peripherals.

Power Supplies

Conversion and form factors

Wall outlets supply AC; computer components need lower-voltage DC. The PSU performs this conversion.

  • AT: original legacy type; obsolete
  • ATX: updated AT; also obsolete
  • ATX12V: most common; adds dedicated CPU power
  • EPS12V: server origin; now also high-end desktops

Voltage and connectors

Digital circuits normally use 3.3 V and 5 V. Drive and fan motors commonly use 12 V.

Supplies can be single-, dual-, or multi-rail. A rail is the internal PCB where external cables connect. Keyed connectors fit only one way. Molex powers drives/devices; 6/8-pin PCIe connectors power demanding parts such as GPUs.

Motherboards

The motherboard—also called the system board or main board—is the computer's backbone. Its printed electrical pathways are buses.

CPU socketRAM slotsExpansion slotsChipsetBIOS/UEFISATA connectorsInternal USB
  • Chipset: controls hardware communication and affects supported memory/connectors.
  • BIOS/UEFI: boots and manages hardware; UEFI is the modern standard.
  • Firmware: programming that lets an OS control hardware.
  • SATA: connects optical drives, HDDs, and SSDs and supports hot swapping—replacement without shutdown.
  • Northbridge: high-speed CPU access to RAM/video. Southbridge: slower devices such as drives, USB, and expansion slots.

CPU and Cooling

The Central Processing Unit interprets and executes hardware and software instructions. Intel and AMD are common manufacturers. The CPU socket joins the processor to the motherboard.

PGA

Pin Grid Array: pins are under the processor. It installs using a ZIF—Zero Insertion Force—socket.

LGA

Land Grid Array: pins are in the motherboard socket instead of on the processor.

Current creates heat. Too much can slow the system, cause crashes, or damage parts. Passive cooling needs no power (heat sink); active cooling does (fan).

Memory

One byte contains 8 bits. ROM is nonvolatile and holds important instructions such as booting. RAM is volatile working storage for CPU programs and data. More RAM can reduce slow transfers to storage; capacity depends on the motherboard.

DRAMSRAMSDRAMDDR–DDR5GDDRSingle/double-sided
  • Single, dual, triple, or quadruple channels can increase bandwidth when supported.
  • SRAM is faster than regular DRAM and generally used for CPU cache. Cache levels are L1, L2, and L3; L1 is CPU-integrated.
  • Nonparity does not check errors; parity detects them; ECC can correct certain errors but not multiple-bit errors.
  • EPROM can be erased with strong ultraviolet light. DIMM supports 32- and 64-bit transfers.

Adapter Cards and Expansion Slots

Cards expand or customize a computer. Examples include sound, wired/wireless NIC, capture, TV tuner, USB controller, video, and eSATA cards. Some functions may be motherboard-integrated.

  • The connector must match the slot.
  • PCI: 32- or 64-bit and mostly obsolete.
  • PCIe: x1, x4, x8, x16; backward-compatible and runs at the lowest component version.
  • Many slots supply up to 25 W; graphics slots may supply 75 W. Powerful GPUs may require extra PSU power.
  • A riser adds slots. Laptop Mini-PCI comes in Type I, II, and III.

Storage

HDD

Magnetic, nonvolatile storage. Speed is measured in RPM: 5400, 7200, 10,000, or 15,000. Sizes include 3.5-inch desktop, 2.5-inch mobile, and 1.8-inch.

SSD

Flash storage with no moving parts: faster, quieter, cooler, more efficient, and generally more reliable. Forms include drive packages, expansion cards, mSATA, and M.2.

NVMe lets SSDs communicate efficiently over PCIe. An SSHD combines a magnetic HDD with flash cache for a speed/cost compromise.

Optical Storage

Optical drives use lasers with CD, DVD, or Blu-ray media. ROM is prerecorded/read-only; R media (CD-R, DVD ±R, Blu-ray-R) is write-once; RW/RE is rewritable.

Ports, Cables, and Adapters

Video ports carry analog, digital, or both signal types. DVI-D is digital, DVI-A analog, and DVI-I both. Other important connectors include HDMI, SATA, Thunderbolt, and USB. I/O ports attach peripherals such as printers, scanners, and portable drives.

An adapter physically joins technologies (DVI-to-HDMI). A converter also translates the signal (USB 3.0-to-SATA).

Input Devices

Input devices let users communicate with computers: keyboards, mice, touch screens, styluses, magnetic-strip readers, barcode scanners, NFC, facial/fingerprint scanners, voice recognition, and VR inputs.

  • The first input type listed in the course quiz is the keyboard.
  • Biometrics are best for secure access.
  • Barcode scanners are common at self-checkout.
  • Smartphones commonly use keyboards, NFC tap pay, microphones, and fingerprint readers.

Output Devices

Output devices turn binary information into forms people understand: monitors, projectors, VR headsets, televisions, printers, speakers, and headphones.

Displays

  • LCD: liquid crystals between polarizing filters.
  • LED: LCD with LED backlighting—lower power, thinner, lighter, brighter, better contrast.
  • OLED: self-lit pixels for deeper black levels.

VR and AR

VR blocks/replaces surroundings with a tracked 3D simulation. Sensors may include cameras, accelerometers, gyroscopes, and magnetometers. AR overlays digital media while the real world remains visible through phones, glasses, or headsets.

Printers create paper or 3D hard copies using ink, toner, or liquid plastic. Inkjet, impact, 3D, and thermal are examples. Maintain hardware and update drivers. Speakers/headphones provide auditory output through integrated or adapter-card audio.
